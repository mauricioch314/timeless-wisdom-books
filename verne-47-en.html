Second Fatherland

Published in 1900, Second Fatherland (original French: Seconde patrie) is Jules Verne's expansive sequel to Johann David Wyss's classic novel The Swiss Family Robinson. Decades after the original shipwreck that cast the Zermatt family onto a deserted island, the novel returns to that same shore — but not in triumph. It is the weight of nostalgia that draws them back, the longing for a place that was once a prison and became a home.

Pastor Zermatt, the patriarch of the family, has raised his children — Fritz, Ernest, and Jenny — in the island paradise they named New Switzerland. The years of isolation have forged a self-sufficient community, a testament to human ingenuity and familial devotion. But when the family finally has the opportunity to return to Europe, they discover that civilization no longer feels like home. The call of the island, with its pristine beaches, its known dangers, and its accumulated memories, proves irresistible.

Their return to New Switzerland is not a simple homecoming. The island has changed in their absence, and new threats — both natural and human — test the bonds they formed on its shores. Fritz, the eldest son and natural leader, must confront challenges that demand the courage and resourcefulness he developed during the original shipwreck. Ernest, the thoughtful second son, brings a more measured perspective to the trials that await them. Jenny, the daughter, embodies the family's spirit of resilience and adaptability.

Verne transforms Wyss's family adventure into a deeper meditation on belonging, exile, and the meaning of home. Second Fatherland asks whether a place can truly become a native land through sheer love and labor, and whether the bonds forged in adversity can survive the return to ordinary life. It is a novel about the geography of the heart — about the island that exists not on any map, but in the memories of those who lived there.

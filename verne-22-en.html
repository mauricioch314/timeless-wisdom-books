Godfrey Morgan

Published in 1882, Godfrey Morgan (also known as A Castaway on a Desert Island or The Robinson Crusoe School) is Jules Verne's playful and affectionate contribution to the castaway tradition. It blends humor, adventure, and satire into a story about the difference between learning about life and actually living it.

Godfrey Morgan is a young man of comfortable means, engaged to his devoted cousin Phina, yet restless for adventure before settling into domestic life. His uncle, a man of practical wisdom, decides to grant his wish by sending him on a voyage around the world — accompanied, to Godfrey's dismay, by Professor Tartlet, his earnest tutor and athletics coach. The journey is meant to be instructive, a grand tour that will prepare Godfrey for responsibility.

But the ship is wrecked, and Godfrey, Tartlet, and a sailor named Will find themselves stranded on a deserted island. Suddenly, all the theory Godfrey has absorbed from books must be translated into practice: building shelter, finding food, making fire, and surviving. The comic contrast between Tartlet's academic pedagogy and the raw demands of survival becomes one of the novel's great pleasures.

Godfrey Morgan is a warm and engaging novel about the education of character. Verne gently mocks the notion that life can be learned from books alone, while celebrating the resourcefulness and growth that come from genuine experience. It is a story about becoming the person you were meant to be — even if the path there involves shipwrecks, crabs, and a very confused professor.
